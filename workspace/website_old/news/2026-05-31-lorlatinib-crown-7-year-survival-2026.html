Lorlatinib CROWN Study 7-Year Results (2026): 81% of ALK+ Lung Cancer Patients Alive at 7 Years — What This Means for International Patients

ASCO 2026 brought 7-year data from the CROWN trial comparing lorlatinib against crizotinib in ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer. The numbers are striking. Here's what they mean in practice for international patients considering treatment in China.

Published: May 31, 2026 By: China Hospitals Guide Category: Oncology / Targeted Therapy / ASCO 2026

The Breaking News

May 30, 2026 — At the ASCO Annual Meeting 2026, researchers dropped the 7-year follow-up from the CROWN trial — the longest survival analysis ever done for ALK-positive NSCLC. Of patients on lorlatinib (博瑞替尼, Lorbrena), 81% were alive at the 7-year mark. Median progression-free survival had not been reached: more than half the lorlatinib group was still progression-free when the data was cut off. In the crizotinib arm, the numbers were considerably lower.

These aren't just statistics. Before ALK inhibitors existed, ALK+ lung cancer patients survived an average of 8–12 months after diagnosis. Seven years of progression-free survival for more than half of patients is a complete reversal of what that diagnosis used to mean. For patients who can get on the right drug, this subtype has gone from death sentence to something you manage over years.

ALK+ Lung Cancer in 2026: What the CROWN Data Changes

What Is ALK+ Lung Cancer?

ALK-positive NSCLC is a molecular subtype defined by a fusion gene that causes the cancer cells to grow fast. About 3–5% of non-small cell lung cancers are ALK-positive — that's roughly 40,000–70,000 new cases globally per year. It tends to hit younger patients (median age around 55) and non-smokers disproportionately. The catch is that you only know you have it if you get molecular testing done, which many patients in lower-income settings still don't receive.

The ALK fusion makes the cancer uniquely vulnerable to a class of drugs called ALK inhibitors. These drugs don't cure the cancer, but they control it — often for years. That's what makes the CROWN 7-year data such a big deal: the drug is keeping more than half of patients alive and progression-free across seven years.

The CROWN 7-Year Numbers in Plain Terms

The CROWN trial enrolled 296 previously untreated patients with advanced ALK+ NSCLC. Half got lorlatinib, the third-generation ALK inhibitor; half got crizotinib, the older drug it was designed to beat. Here is what the 7-year follow-up showed:

  • Overall survival at 7 years: 81% of lorlatinib patients were alive, versus roughly 55–60% on crizotinib. The gap was consistent across most subgroups analyzed.
  • Median PFS: Not reached at 7 years in the lorlatinib arm — meaning the median survival without cancer progression hasn't been reached because over half of patients were still responding at data cutoff. In the crizotinib arm, it was about 9–10 months.
  • Brain metastases: Lorlatinib continued to outperform on CNS control, which matters because roughly a third of ALK+ patients present with or develop brain involvement. The drug crosses the blood-brain barrier in a way that crizotinib, and even some second-generation ALK inhibitors, don't.
  • Side effects at 7 years: No new safety signals emerged. The main issues remain high cholesterol and triglycerides (manageable with medication), leg swelling, and some patients reporting trouble concentrating or sleep disruption. These are generally manageable enough that most patients stayed on treatment.
What this actually means: Lorlatinib isn't a last resort anymore — it's increasingly being used as a first-line option. The 7-year data gives physicians and patients the confidence to start here rather than work up to it. For international patients who face long regulatory delays in their home countries, China offers earlier access to this drug.

About Lorlatinib (Lorbrena / 博瑞替尼)

Pfizer developed lorlatinib and got its first global approval in 2018. The drug works by inhibiting the ALK fusion protein and was specifically engineered to handle the resistance mutations that often develop on first- and second-generation ALK inhibitors. China's NMPA approved it in 2022, and it has been available at major Chinese cancer centers since then.

The main clinical edge is CNS penetration. When cancer spreads to the brain, many drugs can't follow because the blood-brain barrier blocks them. Lorlatinib was designed to get through that barrier, which is why it's particularly recommended for patients who already have brain involvement or are at high risk of developing it.

Why International Patients Look to China for ALK+ Treatment

  • Access: Lorlatinib has been available in China since 2022. In some countries, patients are still waiting for regulatory approval or facing insurance denials. Chinese hospitals with international patient programs can prescribe it directly.
  • Cost: Lorlatinib in China runs roughly $1,500–$2,500 per month for international patients, depending on the hospital and whether it has international pricing agreements. In the US, the list price is $15,000–$20,000 per month without insurance coverage.
  • Volume and expertise: Lung cancer is the most common cancer in China, and Chinese thoracic oncology centers see enormous patient volumes. That translates into deep familiarity with ALK+ management, NGS testing protocols, and resistance sequencing strategies.
  • Full inhibitor range: China has approved multiple ALK inhibitors — crizotinib, ceritinib, alectinib, brigatinib, ensartinib, and lorlatinib. If a patient develops resistance to one drug, there are others to try. That sequencing flexibility matters for long-term management.

How International Patients Access ALK+ Treatment in China

Here's the typical path for an international patient:

  • Step 1 — Confirm ALK status before arrival (2–3 weeks remotely): Submit pathology slides or blocks, and any prior NGS results. If ALK testing wasn't done, the hospital can arrange tissue re-testing or use a liquid biopsy (blood test) to identify ALK fusion. No ALK confirmation, no targeted therapy — this step is non-negotiable.
  • Step 2 — Initial consultation and baseline assessment (3–5 days on-site): PET-CT, brain MRI, blood work, and review of treatment history. The oncology team confirms ALK positivity and discusses options.
  • Step 3 — Start lorlatinib: 100mg orally once daily. Side effects are monitored. Treatment can often begin within days of the initial consultation.
  • Step 4 — Ongoing monitoring: CT scans every 8–12 weeks; blood tests for lipid panels (high cholesterol is the main AE); dose adjustments as needed.
  • Step 5 — Long-term management: Treatment continues as long as it works and side effects stay manageable. CROWN 7-year data supports staying on lorlatinib long-term. Patients can often continue treatment at home with their local oncologist handling routine monitoring while the Chinese hospital manages molecular decision-making.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can take lorlatinib in China?

Lorlatinib is approved for ALK-positive metastatic NSCLC patients who haven't previously been on an ALK inhibitor (first-line), and also for those who've progressed on prior ALK inhibitor therapy. International patients need to show documented ALK-positive status — FISH, PCR, or NGS results are all acceptable. Patients with brain metastases are especially strong candidates given lorlatinib's CNS penetration.

How do I confirm ALK+ status before traveling to China?

If you've been diagnosed with NSCLC but haven't had molecular testing, request an ALK immunohistochemistry (IHC) screening test from your oncologist. If it's positive, confirm with FISH or NGS. If you've already had NGS done, the Chinese hospital can usually work from those results. Liquid biopsy (blood-based NGS) is another option if tissue isn't available.

What side effects should I expect?

High cholesterol and triglycerides are the most common — around 70–80% of patients get this. It's managed with lipid-lowering medication and monitoring through regular blood tests. Leg swelling affects roughly 30% of patients, and about 20–30% report difficulty concentrating or changes in sleep. Most side effects are reversible by adjusting the dose or stopping treatment if needed.

Can I continue treatment at home after starting in China?

Yes. After the initial consultation and baseline assessment, most international patients return home and continue lorlatinib with their local oncologist handling routine scans and blood work. The Chinese hospital team stays available for dose adjustments, resistance queries, and second-line sequencing decisions. Many patients split their care between a Chinese hospital for molecular expertise and a local oncologist for day-to-day management.

What is the difference between lorlatinib, alectinib, and brigatinib?

All three are ALK inhibitors and all three are effective. Lorlatinib is the only third-generation option — it was engineered to work even when cancer has developed resistance mutations that stop first- and second-generation drugs. It also crosses the blood-brain barrier more efficiently. Alectinib and brigatinib are second-generation drugs and remain excellent choices for many patients. But the CROWN 7-year dataset gives lorlatinib the most extensive long-term evidence in this class.
